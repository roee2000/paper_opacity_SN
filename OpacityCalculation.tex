\title{Rosseland Mean Opacity for Expansion Opacity from Bound-Bound Lines} %% Article title

%% Abstract
\begin{abstract}
We present a new algorithm for calculating Rosseland mean opacity that accounts for expansion opacity effects from bound-bound atomic transitions. The method smooths the frequency-dependent opacity using a $\tau=1$ window, where for each frequency the distance required to reach optical depth unity is calculated. The smoothed opacity is then determined from $\kappa = 1/(\rho \Delta x)$, where $\Delta x$ is the path length for $\tau=1$. Monte Carlo radiative transfer simulations using this new Rosseland opacity yield results identical to full frequency-group calculations, demonstrating that the averaging successfully captures the essential physics of expansion opacity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{SecIntro}

The interaction between radiation and matter is primarily determined by the material properties such as composition, density,
and temperature, as well as the spectral characteristics of the radiation.
In expanding plasmas, due to the Doppler effect, as photons travel through the plasma, their frequency changes. 
This effect is mainly important for bound-bound transitions, due to their high gradient in frequency which cause photons to interact 
with different lines as they travel through the plasma.
This effect is known as expansion opacity, and it is important for many astrophysical applications, such as Type Ia supernovae
and neutron star mergers. 
Since in most applications, the velocity of the plasma is no more the few percents of the speed of light, the frequency shift due
to the Doppler shift is approximately $\Delta \nu = \nu_0 \frac{\Delta v}{c}$.

For non-moving plasmas, the radiation flux can be usually approximated well using the Rosseland mean opacity, which is a weighted average
of the opacity over all frequencies. However, for expanding plasmas, the Rosseland mean opacity is not a good approximation,
since the opacity changes as the photon travels through the plasma. And therefore the average distance a photon travels depends on 
his direction of movement and the plasma's velocity gradient.

In this paper we present a new algorithm for calculating Rosseland mean opacity that properly accounts for expansion opacity 
effects from bound-bound lines. The method assume a photon which travels "outwards" from the center of the expanding plasma and assumes
bolometric expansion ($r=v\cdot t $) and thus gives the opacity as a function of time in addition to the density and temperature $ \kappa (T, \rho, t) $.

The structure of the paper is as follows: In Section \ref{SecExpansion} we describe the expansion opacity and how it is calculated.
In Section XXX, we discuss the effective Rosseland mean opacity calculation for expansion opacity.
In Section \ref{SecRosseland} we describe the results given by the algorithm and discuess their physical meaning. 
In Section \ref{SecValidation} we validate the algorithm using Monte Carlo radiative transfer simulations. 
In Section \ref{SecSummary} we summarize the results and discuss future work.

\section{Expansion Opacity}
\label{SecExpansion}

The total opacity $\kappa_{\mathrm{tot}}$ is the sum of contributions from different physical processes:
\begin{equation}
\kappa_{\mathrm{tot}} = \kappa_{\mathrm{th}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{ff}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{bf}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{bb}}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_{\mathrm{th}}$ is Thomson scattering, $\kappa_{\mathrm{ff}}$ is free-free opacity, $\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}$ is 
bound-free opacity (which we neglect in this work), and $\kappa_{\mathrm{bb}}$ is bound-bound opacity from atomic line 
transitions.

The BB opacity $\kappa_{\mathrm{bb}}$ depends on the plasma's ionization and excitation populations for each material. This is 
calculated using the Boltzmann distribution.

\begin{equation}
N_{m,i+1} = \frac{N_{m,i}}{N_{m,0}} \frac{Z_{m,i+1}}{Z_{m,i}} \exp\left(-\frac{E_{i+1}-E_i}{kT}\right) \cdot g_{e}
\end{equation}
where $m, i, j$ are the indexes of the material, ionization level and energy level respectively, $N$ is the number density, $g$
and $E$ are the degeneracy and energy of the energy level respectively, $k$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T$ is the temperature and 
$g_e$ is the constant factor $g_e = \left(\frac{2\pi m_e kT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}$.

$Z_{m,i}$ is the partition function of the state $i$ for the material $m$, and is calculated using the partition function formula:
\begin{equation}
Z_{m,i} = \sum_{j} g_j \exp\left(-\frac{E_j}{kT}\right)
\end{equation}

The number density $N_{m, i}$ is the calculated using the Saha equation.
\begin{equation}
\frac{N_{m, i}}{N_{m, 0}} = \frac{g_i}{g_0} \left(\frac{2\pi m_e kT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2} \exp\left(-\frac{E_i}{kT}\right)
\end{equation}
where $m_e$ is the electron mass.

The opactical depth of the bound-bound line is then calculated using the Sobolev approximation.
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{j_l,j_u} = t \cdot N_{m,i,j_l} \cdot f_{j_l,j_u} \cdot \frac{\pi e^2}{m_e c}
\end{equation}
where $j_l$ and $j_u$ are the lower and upper energy levels respectively and $f_{j_l,j_u}$ is the oscillator strength of the transition 
from the energy level $j_l$ to the energy level $j_u$, $t$ is the time from explosion.

Then, the opacity is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_{BB}(\nu) = \sum_l \delta(\nu - \nu_{l}) \cdot \tau_{l}
\end{equation}

For a steady plasma, the rosseland mean opacity is giving the average distance energy travels before being scattered or 
absorbed. The rosseland mean opacity is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{\kappa_{\mathrm{R}}} = \frac{\int_0^\infty \kappa_\nu^-1 \frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T} d\nu}{\int_0^\infty \frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T} d\nu}
\end{equation}
where $B_\nu$ is the Planck function and $T$ is the temperature. 

The naive application of Rosseland mean opacity to expansion opacity does not work for medium with high BB contribution 
because BB lines have no width and therefore their contribution is zero.

Therefore, up to now, the radiation transport for expansding systems with significant BB opacity was calculated 
by tracking photons with Monte Carlo simulations. The photon's frequnecy was changed according to the Doppler shift,
and each time a photon reached a new spectral line, the photon was absorbed according to the probability for absorption
which is $P = 1 - \exp(-\tau_{j_l,j_u})$. 

A outward travelling photon with frequency $\nu_0$, will reach a new spectral line with frequency $\nu_{l}$ after a velocity
shift of $\Delta v = -c \ln(\nu_0/\nu_{l})$ which is corresponding to a distance of $\Delta x = c t \ln(\nu_0/\nu_{l})$.

where $\Delta x$ is the path length over which $\tau = 1$ is reached. This approach effectively averages the opacity over a 
window defined by the expansion physics, capturing the essential behavior of bound-bound line transitions in expanding media.

When using large frequency bins, if the distance required to reach $\tau \geq 1$ is less than a single frequency bin width, 
the effective mean free path after saturation is calculated according to:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{1}{\rho \kappa_{\mathrm{eff}}}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1/(\rho \Delta x)$ and $\Delta x$ is the path length over which $\tau = 1$ is reached within the 
bin. This provides a practical way to estimate the effective opacity in each bin when the optical depth saturates before 
crossing the bin boundary, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:BB_line_example}.

\section{Rosseland Mean from Smoothed Opacity}
\label{SecRosseland}

The standard Rosseland mean opacity is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\kappa_R} = \frac{\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{\kappa_\nu} \frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T} d\nu}{\int_0^\infty \frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T} d\nu}
\end{equation}

where $B_\nu$ is the Planck function and $T$ is the temperature. Using the smoothed opacity $\kappa_{\mathrm{smooth}}(\nu)$ 
from the $\tau=1$ window algorithm, we compute the expansion-corrected Rosseland mean:

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\kappa_{R,\mathrm{exp}}} = \frac{\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{\kappa_{\mathrm{smooth}}(\nu)} \frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T} d\nu}{\int_0^\infty \frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T} d\nu}
\end{equation}

\section{Validation}
\label{SecValidation}

Monte Carlo radiative transfer simulations using the new Rosseland mean opacity calculated from the $\tau=1$ window algorithm 
yield results identical to full frequency-group calculations. The simulations were performed using URILIGHT \cite{urilight1,urilight2}, 
an open-source Monte Carlo radiative transfer code designed for supernova ejecta. URILIGHT solves the time-dependent radiative 
transfer equation in expanding media using a Monte Carlo approach, tracking photon packets through the ejecta and accounting 
for bound-bound, bound-free, and free-free opacity sources. This demonstrates that the averaging process successfully captures 
the essential physics of expansion opacity, allowing for efficient gray opacity calculations without loss of accuracy.

\section{Summary}
\label{SecSummary}

We have presented a new algorithm for calculating Rosseland mean opacity that accounts for expansion opacity effects from 
bound-bound atomic transitions. The method uses a $\tau=1$ window to smooth the frequency-dependent opacity, with the smoothed 
value calculated as $\kappa = 1/(\rho \Delta x)$ where $\Delta x$ is the path length to reach optical depth unity. Validation 
through Monte Carlo simulations confirms that this approach captures the essential physics while enabling efficient gray 
opacity calculations.
