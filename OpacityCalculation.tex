\title{Defining an Effective Rosseland Mean for Expansion Opacity} %% Article title

%% Abstract
\begin{abstract}
The standard Rosseland mean does not account for the Doppler shift of photons through spectral lines during expansion
and can substantially misestimate the opacity of line-rich ejecta.
For each frequency, we define an effective expansion opacity from the distance an outward
travelling photon covers before reaching optical depth unity,
$\kappa_{\rm exp}=1/(\rho\Delta x)$. Its Rosseland mean, $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$, provides a gray
transport opacity for expanding media where bound-bound transitions dominate.
For kilonova ejecta, this method allows us to determine the effective opacity as a function of
lanthanide mass fraction $X_{\rm lan}$. Using an eight-element composition derived from the solar
$r$-process pattern, we obtain the approximate effective Rosseland opacity
$\kappa_{\rm avg}\simeq0.34+99X_{\rm lan}^{0.72}$~cm$^2$/g during the diffusion-dominated phase,
for the adopted composition and atomic data.
Single-group Monte Carlo simulations using this opacity reproduce the trends of full frequency-group
calculations for Type~Ia supernova and kilonova ejecta, with kilonova peak luminosities differing
by $7$--$33\%$. For lanthanide-rich kilonova ejecta, the single-group calculation is faster by around 2 
orders of magnitude.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{SecIntro}

    The interaction between radiation and matter is primarily determined by the material properties such as composition, density,
    and temperature, as well as the spectral characteristics of the radiation.
    In expanding plasmas, due to the Doppler effect, as photons travel through the plasma, their frequency changes. 
    This effect is mainly important for bound-bound transitions, because their narrow line structure in frequency causes photons to interact 
    with different lines as they travel through the plasma.
    This effect is known as expansion opacity, and it is important for many astrophysical applications, such as Type Ia supernovae,
    kilonovae and stellar winds. 
    %Under the reasonable condition of non-ultra relativistic velocities, 
    %the frequency shift due to the Doppler shift is approximately $\Delta \nu = \nu_0 \frac{\Delta v}{c}$.
    %to write its usually less then 10\% of the frequency.

    For non-moving plasmas, the radiation flux can be approximated well using the Rosseland mean opacity, which is a weighted
    average of the opacity over all frequencies. However, for expanding plasmas with significant bound-bound opacity, the Rosseland mean 
    opacity is not a good approximation, since the width of the bb lines is negligible and photons interact with them only due to their
    frequency change as they travel through the plasma. 
    Therefore the average distance a photon travels cannot be derived from $\kappa(\nu)$
    alone because it inherently depends on the photon's direction of movement and the plasma's velocity gradient.
    To avoid this problem and to allow calculation of transport that can inherently depend on the photon's direction of movement and
    velocity gradient, radiation transport in systems with expansion opacity is usually calculated using Monte Carlo simulations,
    which track individual photons and their frequency evolution as they propagate through the expanding medium.

    Calculating an effective Rosseland mean opacity has several motivations: a single opacity value 
    (1) is required for analytic 
    models and closed-form solutions, which cannot treat full frequency-dependent opacity; (2) gives direct physical 
    intuition for how temperature and composition affect the opacity and hence the light curve; and (3) enables 
    faster and simpler simulations than full frequency-dependent calculations. 

    Previous attempts to address this problem have been proposed. Fontes et al. \cite{fontes2015opacity,fontes2017linesmeared}
    suggested using artificial 
    line broadening by assigning a line width corresponding to unphysical high temperature of 20 MeV, 
    which effectively smears the bound-bound lines 
    over a finite frequency range. This approach preserves the integral of the opacity over
    frequency (area-preserving line profiles) and for NSM ejecta it  
    produces frequency-dependent opacities that are one to two orders of magnitude greater than those
    obtained with expansion opacities in 
    the Sobolev approximation without broadening. Fontes et al. \cite{fontes2020opacity} demonstrated that
    their smeared opacity tables, 
    calculated using the Sobolev formalism, yield light curves for multigroup radiative transfer simulations
    that are similar to those 
    obtained from full line-by-line Monte Carlo radiation transport (using the superNu code) for kilonovae (neutron
    star mergers).
    However, this approach still requires multigroup frequency-dependent opacity tables rather than
    a single gray (frequency-averaged) Rosseland mean opacity and uses an unphysical assumption that
    has been verified only for specific ejecta.
    Several works \cite{deprince2024kilonova,just2022properties,tanaka2020systematic} have shown how opacity depends on composition,
    temperature, density, and time by comparing the Planck mean expansion opacity over those parameters.
    The Planck mean is nevertheless not the appropriate average for the opacity that controls photon
    escape from the ejecta. In a non-expanding medium this transport opacity is the Rosseland mean,
    which weights the low-opacity frequency windows through which photons preferentially diffuse.
    In an expanding medium the same transport character is needed, but the opacity must also include
    the line absorption encountered as the photon redshifts through the expanding flow.

    In this paper we present the effective expansion opacity, $\kappa_{\rm exp}$, which accounts for
    bound-bound expansion opacity. The method follows an outward-moving photon in homologous expansion
    ($r=vt$) and calculates the distance over which it reaches optical depth $\tau\simeq1$.
    Taking the Rosseland mean of $\kappa_{\rm exp}$ defines the effective expansion Rosseland mean,
    $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}(T,\rho,t)$.
    This gray-opacity method assumes that the photons dominating the diffusion are close to a
    blackbody spectrum. It is therefore useful for bolometric luminosities and characteristic
    temperatures, but not for detailed line spectra.

    The structure of the paper is as follows: In \secref{SecExpansion} we describe how the expansion opacity is calculated, 
    and define the effective expansion opacity.
    In \secref{SecRosseland}, we show the variation of $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$ with composition, temperature, density, and time
    and show how it scales differently from the Planck mean.
    In \secref{SecRadiationSimulations} we demonstrate the use of $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$ in radiative transfer simulations and compare the
    results to the full frequency-group calculations.
    In \secref{SecTypicalOpacity} we determine the effective opacity sampled by the diffusion
    region and derive its dependence on the kilonova lanthanide fraction.
    In \secref{SecSummary} we summarize the results.

\section{Expansion Opacity}
    \label{SecExpansion}
    
    The total opacity $\kappa$ is the sum of contributions from different physical processes:
    \begin{equation}
    \kappa = \kappa_{\mathrm{T}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{ff}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{bf}} + \kappa_{\mathrm{bb}}
    \end{equation}
    where $\kappa_{\mathrm{T}}$ is Thomson scattering, $\kappa_{\mathrm{ff}}$ is free-free opacity, $\kappa_{\mathrm{bf}}$ is 
    bound-free opacity (which we neglect in this work), and $\kappa_{\mathrm{bb}}$ is bound-bound opacity from atomic line 
    transitions.

    The bound-bound opacity $\kappa_{\mathrm{bb}}$ depends on the ionization and excitation populations
    of the material. We obtain the ionization fractions from the Saha equation and the level
    populations from the Boltzmann distribution, and use them to calculate the optical depth of each line.

    The optical depth of the bound-bound line is then calculated using the Sobolev approximation \cite{eastmanpinto1993}.
    \begin{equation}
        \tau_{j_l,j_u} = \frac{\pi e^{2}}{m_{e}} \frac{f_{j_l,j_u}}{\nu_{j_l,j_u}} t \left(N_{m,i,j_l} - \frac{g_{j_l}}{g_{j_u}} N_{m,i,j_u}\right)
    \end{equation}
    where $m$ is the material index, $i$ is the ionization state, $j_l$ and $j_u$ are the lower and upper energy levels respectively,
    $f_{j_l,j_u}$ is the oscillator strength of the transition 
    from the energy level $j_l$ to the energy level $j_u$, $\nu_{j_l,j_u}$ is the frequency of the transition, $N_{m,i,j_l}$ and $N_{m,i,j_u}$ 
    are the number densities of the lower and upper levels respectively, $g_{j_l}$ and $g_{j_u}$ are their degeneracies, and $t$ is the time from explosion.

    We use two forms of the bound-bound opacity, saturated and unsaturated:
    \begin{equation}
        \kappa_{bb,saturated}(\nu) = \sum_l \frac{\nu_l}{\rho c t} \delta(\nu - \nu_{l}) \cdot (1 - \exp(-\tau_{j_l,j_u}))
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        \kappa_{bb,unsaturated}(\nu) = \sum_l \frac{\nu_l}{\rho c t} \delta(\nu - \nu_{l}) \cdot \tau_{j_l,j_u}
    \end{equation}

    The saturated opacity gives the contribution of a narrow line to a larger finite frequency bin,
    by capping its effect once it becomes optically thick, since a single line has a maximal effect on the opacity
    once all photons in the line's frequency are absorbed.
    The unsaturated opacity gives the optical depth accumulated by a photon that crosses the
    full frequency bin.

    We define the effective expansion opacity, $\kappa_{\rm exp}$, from the distance an outward travelling photon
    moves through the plasma before accumulating optical depth unity.
    The effective expansion Rosseland mean, $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$, is then the Rosseland mean of
    $\kappa_{\rm exp}$.
    An outward travelling photon with frequency $\nu_0$ will reach a new spectral line with frequency 
    $\nu_{l}$ after a velocity shift of $\Delta v = -c \ln(\nu_0/\nu_{l})$ which corresponds to a
    distance of $\Delta x = -c t \ln(\nu_0/\nu_{l})$.

    For that we define the cumulative frequency optical depth as:
    \begin{equation}
        \tau(\nu) = \int \kappa(\nu) \rho \, dx = ct \int \kappa(\nu) \rho \frac{d\nu}{\nu}
    \end{equation}
    For each frequency $\nu_i$, given the frequency $\nu_j$ such that $\tau(\nu_j) - \tau(\nu_i) = 1$,
    we define the effective expansion opacity as:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:kappa_eff_simple}
        \kappa_{\rm exp}(\nu_i) = \frac{1}{\rho \Delta x}
        = \frac{1}{\rho ct \ln(\nu_j/\nu_i)}
    \end{equation}

    For finite size frequency bins this process is done by estimating where inside the stopping
    bin the photon reaches optical depth unity. This is needed because assigning the stopping
    point to the edge of a frequency bin would make the minimum travel distance depend on the
    chosen bin width, rather than on the line opacity inside the bin.
    
    Numerically, after discretizing the frequency, the stopping bin is found with the unsaturated
    optical depth by finding the first bin $j$ such that
    \begin{equation}
        \tau(\nu_{j-1}) - \tau(\nu_i) < 1,
        \qquad
        \tau(\nu_j) - \tau(\nu_i) > 1
    \end{equation}
    Unsaturated opacity is used for this step because it identifies where the photon reaches true
    optical depth unity; with saturated opacity alone, a strong line with $\tau \gg 1$ contributes
    only $1-\exp(-\tau)$ and cannot set the stopping point by itself.
    We then estimate the sub-bin stopping frequency in bin $j$ using the saturated opacity. If the
    saturated optical depth in this bin can supply the remaining optical depth to unity, we use
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
        \Delta\nu_{\tau=1,i} &= \nu_{j-1} - \nu_i + (\nu_j - \nu_{j-1})
        \frac{1 - (\tau(\nu_{j-1})-\tau(\nu_i))}{\Delta\tau^{s}_j},\\
        \nu_{\tau=1,i} &= \nu_i + \Delta\nu_{\tau=1,i}.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    where $\Delta\tau^{s}_j$ is the saturated optical depth of bin $j$. Otherwise, if
    $\Delta\tau^{s}_j < 1 - (\tau(\nu_{j-1})-\tau(\nu_i))$, we set
    $\Delta\nu_{\tau=1,i}=\nu_j-\nu_i$.
    The effective opacity is then calculated from $\Delta x_i = ct\ln(\nu_{\tau=1,i}/\nu_i)$. When a
    single bin dominates, we instead use
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:kappa_eff}
        \kappa_{\rm exp}(\nu_i) = \frac{ \sum_l (1 - \exp(-\tau(\nu_l)))}{\rho ct \ln(\nu_{i+1}/\nu_i)} + \kappa_{\mathrm{T}} + \kappa_{ff}(\nu_i)
    \end{equation}
    This interpolation is approximate, but it is accurate when
    $\Delta\tau \ll 1$ or $\Delta\tau \gg 1$. For the intermediate case, $\Delta\tau \sim 1$, it
    introduces a small error which should mostly cancel on average.

    \figref{fig:BB_line_example} shows the unsaturated opacity, saturated opacity, and effective
    expansion opacity of iron after 3 days, for density $1 \times 10^{-12}$~g/cm$^3$ and temperature

\section{Rosseland Mean of Effective Expansion Opacity}
    \label{SecRosseland}

    The Planck mean opacity is an emission-weighted average, while the Rosseland mean opacity is a 
    transport-weighted harmonic mean:
    \begin{equation}
        \kappa_P = \frac{\int \kappa_\nu B_\nu(T) d\nu}{\int B_\nu(T) d\nu},
        \qquad
        \frac{1}{\kappa_R} =
        \frac{\int \frac{1}{\kappa_\nu} \frac{\partial B_\nu(T)}{\partial T} d\nu}
        {\int \frac{\partial B_\nu(T)}{\partial T} d\nu}.
    \end{equation}
    where $B_\nu(T)$ is the Planck function.
    When the Rosseland average is applied to the usual frequency-dependent opacity $\kappa_\nu$ we denote
    the result by $\kappa_R$. When it is applied to the effective expansion opacity $\kappa_{\rm exp}$,
    we denote the result by $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$.

    The main problem with applying the Rosseland mean directly to the expansion opacity is that zero-width
    bound-bound lines cannot contribute to a frequency-integrated harmonic mean; numerically, this appears as
    a vanishing bound-bound contribution as the frequency bins are refined.
    \figref{fig:convergence} shows the convergence of the Rosseland mean opacities as a function of the
    frequency resolution $\Delta\nu/\nu$ (for logarithmically spaced frequency bins)
    for iron (Fe, $Z=26$) in top and erbium (Er, $Z = 68$) in bottom.
    Iron is an iron-group element with an open d-block, representative of the heavy material in
    Type~Ia supernova ejecta. Erbium is a lanthanide with an open f-block, which is common in
    kilonova compositions and gives a much larger bound-bound opacity than iron.
    In \figref{fig:convergence} we plot the ordinary Rosseland mean of the saturated expansion opacity,
    $\kappa_R$, and the effective expansion Rosseland mean, $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$.

    The results show that $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$ is barely dependent on the frequency resolution
    and gives converged opacity even for low resolutions such as 
    $\Delta\nu/\nu \gtrsim 4\cdot 10^{-2}$ (which is equivalent to less than 200 bins for the full spectrum used).
    The reason the effective opacity gives correct results even for large bins is that the spectral lines are
    randomly distributed within each frequency bin. As long as the lines are randomly 
    distributed, \eqnref{eq:kappa_eff} provides a good approximation since it represents the typical distance
    a photon travels in a frequency bin. The fact that we observe good agreement between low-resolution and
    high-resolution calculations for $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$ for both iron and erbium confirms 
    that the line locations are indeed close enough to randomly distributed within large bins. 
    This also offers a rationale for the success of the artificial line-broadening prescription
    of Fontes et al.\ \cite{fontes2015opacity,fontes2017linesmeared}:
    spreading line opacity over frequency is analogous to many narrow lines at random positions within
    a coarse bin, so either picture can yield similar cumulative opacity in the bin, 
    consistent with smeared Sobolev tables reproducing Monte Carlo transport \cite{fontes2020opacity}.
    At low frequency resolution $\kappa_R$ of the saturated expansion opacity and
    $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$ are close because a single wide bin often sets the stopping distance. In that limit,
    \eqnref{eq:kappa_eff} applies and the effective opacity is the same as the saturated opacity.
    Using the formalism described above, we calculated the opacity for different times,
    densities, and temperatures and constructed tables for Type~Ia supernova and kilonova
    simulations.
    To simulate Type~Ia supernova ejecta, we created two tables for two compositions.
    The first for the outer layer of the supernova, which is a mixture of Silicon, Sulfur, Argon, and Calcium 
    in the ratio of 0.535, 0.32, 0.062, and 0.083 respectively.
    %Si: 0.535, S: 0.32, Ar: 0.062, Ca: 0.083
    The second represents the supernova core and follows the $^{56}$Ni decay chain,
    $^{56}{\rm Ni}\rightarrow{}^{56}{\rm Co}\rightarrow{}^{56}{\rm Fe}$, using the composition appropriate
    for each time window. We assumed the half-lives of 
    $t_{1/2}(^{56}{\rm Ni}) = 6.075$ days and $t_{1/2}(^{56}{\rm Co}) = 77.233$ days.
    Kilonova ejecta contain essentially every $r$-process species, and computing a line list for
    each of them is impractical. We therefore built the kilonova table for a reduced mixture of
    eight representative elements derived directly from the solar $r$-process abundance pattern.
    We start from the solar $r$-process residuals $N_r(A)$, i.e.\ the solar isotopic abundances
    with the calculated $s$-process contribution subtracted, taken from Cowan et al.\
    \cite{cowan2006nucleosynthesis}.
    Converting to mass, $M(A)\propto A\,N_r(A)$, we restrict the sum to $90<A\leq215$,
    which holds $20\%$ of the mass of the tabulated pattern ($69\leq A\leq238$) and covers the
    second ($A\approx130$) and third ($A\approx195$) $r$-process peaks together with the
    lanthanides between them.
    This range is then divided into eight contiguous windows in $A$, each represented by the one
    element whose standard atomic weight falls inside it, and each representative is given the
    mass fraction accumulated in its own window. \figref{fig:kilonova_composition} shows the
    cumulative mass as a function of $A$ with the window boundaries and the resulting fractions.
    The eight representatives are Zr ($Z=40$), Ru ($44$), Xe ($54$), Nd ($60$), Eu ($63$),
    Er ($68$), Re ($75$), and Pb ($82$), with mass fractions
    $0.049$, $0.108$, $0.460$, $0.043$, $0.030$, $0.047$, $0.206$, and $0.057$.
    Splitting by valence shell, the mixture is $51.7\%$ $p$-block, $36.2\%$ $d$-block, and
    $12.1\%$ $f$-block, so the lanthanide mass fraction of the solar-derived mixture is
    $X_{\rm lan}=0.121$.
    Because $X_{\rm lan}$ is the quantity that varies most strongly between kilonova ejecta
    components, we also built six rescaled tables in which the three lanthanides are scaled to a
    prescribed $X_{\rm lan}$ between $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-1}$, keeping their relative proportions,
    and the five remaining elements are renormalized to $1-X_{\rm lan}$ keeping theirs.
    These tables are used in \secref{SecRadiationSimulations}.

    Each table stores, at every $(T,\rho,t)$, $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$, $f_{\rm abs}$,
    $\kappa_P^{\rm abs}$, and $\kappa_P^{\rm tot}$.
    The fraction $f_{\rm abs}$ is the Rosseland mean of
    $\kappa_{\rm abs}/(\kappa_{\rm abs}+\kappa_{\rm scat})$ with
    $\kappa_{\rm abs}=\kappa_{\rm exp}-\kappa_{\rm T}$ and $\kappa_{\rm scat}=\kappa_{\rm T}$,
    \begin{equation}
        f_{\rm abs} =
        \frac{\int \frac{\kappa_{\rm abs}}{\kappa_{\rm abs}+\kappa_{\rm scat}}
        \frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T}\,d\nu}
        {\int \frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T}\,d\nu}.
    \end{equation}
    In transport we use $\kappa_R^{\rm abs}=f_{\rm abs}\,\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$ and
    $\kappa_R^{\rm scat}=(1-f_{\rm abs})\,\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$: absorption deposits energy and
    re-emits the packet (IMC), while scatter only redirects it.
    The last two entries are Planck means of the saturated opacity and are not used for the same
    purpose. $\kappa_P^{\rm abs}$ is the Planck mean of the saturated absorption opacity,
    $\kappa_{\rm sat}-\kappa_{\rm T}$; it sets the cell temperature, which is chosen so that the
    thermal emission $4\pi\kappa_P^{\rm abs}\rho B(T)$ balances the energy deposited in the cell.
    $\kappa_P^{\rm tot}$ is the Planck mean of the total saturated opacity, including Thomson
    scattering. It takes no part in the transport and is stored only so that the Planck and
    Rosseland weightings can be compared with the same physical processes included in both,
    as we do next.

    Since the ordinary Rosseland mean opacity is problematic to define for expansion opacity, previous works
    \cite{deprince2024kilonova,just2022properties,tanaka2020systematic} often
    used the Planck mean opacity to compare the opacity of different compositions in different temperatures.
    We compare $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$ to the Planck mean through their
    ratio as a function of temperature and density.
    For consistency in this comparison we use the Planck mean of the total saturated opacity,
    $\kappa_P^{\rm tot}$, including Thomson scattering, so that both means contain the same physical
    processes and the ratio tests how much the frequency weighting differs rather than whether
    scattering is included in one mean but not the other.

    For both the Ni-rich and kilonova compositions, where bound-bound lines dominate,
    the opacity tends to decrease with density.
    This trend follows from the structure of
    \eqref{eq:kappa_eff}: each line contributes a blocking factor $1-\exp(-\tau_l)$ divided by
    $\rho c t$, and the Sobolev depth $\tau_l$ is itself proportional to $\rho t$. A weak line with
    $\tau_l\ll1$ therefore contributes in proportion to the number of absorbers, the two density
    factors cancel, and its opacity per unit mass is independent of $\rho$. A strong line with
    $\tau_l\gg1$ instead saturates at $1-\exp(-\tau_l)\to1$: it already blocks its entire frequency
    interval, and adding more absorbers cannot block any more. For strong lines, 
    the total blocked fraction stops growing with density but their opacity contribution falls as $1/\rho$. 

    The ratio between the Planck and Rosseland means varies strongly with temperature and density
    for the Ni-rich core and kilonova composition. At one day, it reaches approximately
    $39$ and $132$, respectively. Therefore, the Planck mean is not a good proxy for the
    expansion Rosseland mean.

    We note that the kilonova figures are cut at $T=2$~eV because our atomic data contain only the first three
    ionization stages, so the mean ionization is capped at $\langle Z\rangle=3$. Above that 
    temperature the material is held artificially at the third stage rather than being stripped
    further, and the opacity is incorrect. This does not affect the transport calculations of
    \secref{SecRadiationSimulations}, because the emergent radiation is set by the diffusion
    surface and not by the optically thick interior: the plasma temperature at that diffusion surface never
    exceeds $1.8$~eV in any of the runs (\figref{fig:kilonova_diffusion_surface}),
    so the opacity that shapes the light curves is always
    taken from within the reliable range, even though the deeply buried cells are assigned
    higher temperatures.


\section{Monte Carlo Simulation With Single Group Opacity}
\label{SecRadiationSimulations}

    We performed radiation transport simulations to test the effective expansion Rosseland opacity model.
    The simulations were performed using URILIGHT \cite{urilightCode,urilight1,urilight2}, a Monte Carlo
    radiative transfer code written by Elbaz et al.\ and designed for supernova ejecta.
    For this work we made several modifications to the code, including support for
    Rosseland and Planck mean opacity tables and related changes needed for the simulations
    presented here, alongside technical changes like parallelization and performance optimization.
    With permission from the code writers, we published the modified version in a Git
    repository (\url{https://github.com/roee2000/Urilight_SN}).
    The Type~Ia supernova model is one-dimensional and spherically
    symmetric, and the ejecta has two composition layers: an inner
    $0.98~M_\odot$ layer of $^{56}{\rm Ni}$ and an outer $0.4~M_\odot$ layer of Si, S, Ar, and Ca.
    The simulation starts at $t=2$~days and ends at $t=80$~days.

    Energy is deposited by the $^{56}{\rm Ni}\rightarrow{}^{56}{\rm Co}\rightarrow{}^{56}{\rm Fe}$
    decay chain through gamma-ray and positron transport. Radiation transport is performed with
    photon packets emitted according to the fraction of the total gamma-ray and positron energy
    deposited in each cell. Existing photon packets are propagated with effective scattering: when a
    packet is absorbed it is not removed, but is re-emitted with a frequency sampled according to the local cell
    emission spectrum.

    The kilonova simulation uses a one-dimensional ejecta profile with total mass $0.05~M_\odot$,
    from $t=0.05$ days to $t=100$ days.
    The composition is the eight-element solar-derived mixture described in
    \secref{SecRosseland}. The heating is
    an external power law per unit mass \cite{korobkin2012kilonova},
    $\dot{\epsilon}=10^{10}(t/1~{\rm day})^{-1.3}~{\rm erg~s^{-1}~g^{-1}}$.
    We repeated the kilonova simulation for the six rescaled compositions, with $X_{\rm lan}$
    between $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-1}$, using the matching opacity table in each case.
    
    In the full frequency-group calculation, photon packets retain their frequency and interact with
    frequency-dependent opacity groups. At each time step the simulation calculates the ionization
    state of each material and processes its bound-bound line list at the local temperature and density.
    In the single-group calculation, the photon mean free path is set by $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$,
    interpolated from the opacity table using local $(T,\rho,t)$.
    The table value $f_{\rm abs}$ splits this into absorbing and scattering Rosseland means
    ($\kappa_R^{\rm abs}$ and $\kappa_R^{\rm scat}$) as described above.
    The cell temperature is determined by requiring the emitted energy to match the
    deposited energy; for this step we use $\kappa_P^{\rm abs}$, the Planck mean of the saturated
    absorption opacity.

    The Type~Ia model uses an exponential density profile matching the W7 model \cite{nomoto1984w7},
    $\rho(v,t)=\rho_{\rm Ia,0}t^{-3}\exp(-v/v_e)$, normalized to $1.38~M_\odot$, with
    $v_e=2.7\times10^8~{\rm cm~s^{-1}}$ ($0.009c$) and an outer edge at $0.093c$.
    The kilonova model has a flat inner profile $\rho=\rho_0t^{-3}$ for $v<0.1c$ and
    $\rho=\rho_0t^{-3}(v/0.1c)^{-4.5}$ for $0.1c<v<0.4c$, following
    \cite{hotokezaka2020macronova}, with $\rho_0$ normalized to a total mass of $0.05~M_\odot$.

    using $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$, to the full frequency-group calculation for both ejecta

\subsection{Runtime Advantage of Single-Group Transport}

    The single-group calculation is faster for three main reasons. First, each photon packet
    samples a Rosseland-mean transport opacity, so fewer packets are needed to obtain a stable
    estimate of the frequency-averaged energy transport. In a multigroup calculation, the same
    average behavior is recovered only statistically from packets distributed over frequency,
    which requires more packets to reduce numerical noise. Second, each packet propagation is
    cheaper. In a line-rich multigroup calculation, photons can repeatedly redshift into dense
    groups of bound-bound lines, be absorbed after very short path lengths, and be re-emitted
    until they enter a more transparent frequency window. These repeated short propagation
    steps add many microsteps inside a single time step. The single-group calculation replaces
    this behavior with an averaged mean free path, so packet histories contain fewer, more
    uniform transport steps. Third, the multigroup calculation must recompute the
    frequency-dependent opacity for every cell from its local $(T,\rho,t)$ by going through the
    bound-bound line list of the material. The single-group calculation instead interpolates
    one value from a precomputed opacity table.

    The speedup depends on which part of the multigroup calculation is the bottleneck.
    For example, for lanthanide-rich kilonova ejecta, where photon transport through many bound-bound lines
    can dominate the runtime, the single-group calculation can be about 60 times faster and has
    smaller numerical noise for the same number of packets.

\section{Typical Effective Rosseland Opacity}
\label{SecTypicalOpacity}

    Analytic, semi-analytic, and simplified transport calculations of kilonovae require a gray
    opacity for each ejecta component
    \cite{villar2017combined,metzger2019kilonovae,collins2023critical,hotokezaka2020macronova,just2022properties}.
    Common choices are $\kappa\simeq0.5$--$1$~cm$^2$/g for lanthanide-poor material and
    $\kappa\simeq10$~cm$^2$/g for lanthanide-rich material. Their widespread use reflects the
    practical need for a constant opacity, even when it is only approximate.

    One approach is to fit the gray opacity to a multigroup light curve or to observations
    \cite{tanaka2013radiative,villar2017combined}. A single constant does not always work.
    Hotokezaka \& Nakar \cite{hotokezaka2020macronova} therefore used different opacities in the
    outer and inner ejecta, while Banerjee et al.\ \cite{banerjee2024diversity} showed that no single
    value reproduces every epoch of a lanthanide-rich multigroup light curve. Just et al.\
    \cite{just2022properties} similarly used an opacity that varies with $X_{\rm lan}$ and $T$.

    Another approach is to use a Planck mean as a proxy for the transport opacity
    \cite{tanaka2020systematic}. This requires choosing representative values of $T$, $\rho$, and
    $t$, and the result is not the Rosseland mean that controls diffusion.

    \subsection{Determining the representative opacity}
    \label{SecRepresentativeOpacity}

    Our method supplies a physically defined gray transport opacity without fitting a light curve
    or selecting a representative thermodynamic point. At each time we locate the diffusion surface
    where $t_{\rm phot}=t$, denoted $t_{\rm diff}=t$ below, and interpolate
    $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}(T,\rho,t)$ in the leaking envelope. We then define the
    effective gray value as the column-mass-weighted Rosseland opacity outside
    $t_{\rm diff}=t$,
    \begin{equation}
        \kappa_{\rm avg}(t)=
        \frac{\displaystyle\int_{v_{\rm diff}}^{v_{\rm max}}
            \kappa_R^{\rm exp}\rho\,t\,dv}
            {\displaystyle\int_{v_{\rm diff}}^{v_{\rm max}}\rho\,t\,dv}
        =\frac{\tau(v_{\rm diff})}{\Sigma(v_{\rm diff})}.
        \label{eq:kappa_avg}
    \end{equation}
    This is the constant opacity that reproduces the same optical depth through the leaking
    envelope at that time while following the temperature, density, and time actually sampled by
    the transport.

    \figref{fig:all_lanthanides_opacity} shows that the opacity decrease near
    $T\simeq1.2$~eV is a nearly universal property of the high-opacity lanthanides in the
    NIST/HFR atomic database adopted here, rather than a peculiarity of the lanthanides in our
    eight-element mixture. At this temperature the triply ionized state (ion IV) becomes dominant.
    Our frequency-dependent calculations for Nd, Eu, and Er show relatively low opacity,
    $\kappa_{\rm exp}\lesssim0.1$~cm$^2$/g, at $h\nu/kT\lesssim2$, even while most of the spectrum
    remains highly opaque. This likely reflects the smaller number of low-frequency lines of the
    triply ionized ions. These low-opacity frequencies strongly reduce the harmonic Rosseland mean
    but have much less influence on the Planck mean. The feature is present for all of
    the lanthanides with large opacity in this database.

    \subsection{A composition-dependent gray-opacity prescription}

    To test whether the inferred values depend strongly on the assumed ejecta profile, we repeated
    the six-composition calculation for outer density slopes $\alpha=-3.5$, $-4.5$, and $-5.5$,
    and for ejecta masses $0.1$ and $10$ times the fiducial mass. For each run we average
    \eqref{eq:kappa_avg} over the time during which
    $0.1c<v_{\rm diff}<0.3c$, weighting uniformly in $\ln t$. This selects the phase during which
    the diffusion surface crosses the power-law envelope and excludes the final transparent phase,
    when the luminosity no longer depends on opacity.

    A single fit in $\ln\kappa$ to all 30 simulations gives
    \begin{equation}
        \boxed{\displaystyle
        \kappa_{\rm avg}(X_{\rm lan}) =
        0.34 + 99\,X_{\rm lan}^{0.72}\ {\rm cm^2\,g^{-1}} .}
        \label{eq:kappa_xlan}
    \end{equation}

    \subsection{Why an approximately constant opacity produces a power-law light curve}

    As shown in \secref{SecRepresentativeOpacity}, the effective opacity remains nearly constant
    through most of the diffusion-dominated phase. We can therefore use a constant-opacity
    analytical model to explain the kilonova luminosity plateau in \figref{fig:comparison}.
    Consider homologous ejecta with gray opacity $\kappa$,
    density $\rho(v,t)=A t^{-3}v^\alpha$, and heating per unit mass
    $\dot{\epsilon}\propto t^{-\beta}$. The optical depth exterior to velocity $v$ is
    \begin{equation}
        \tau(v,t)=\int_v^{v_{\rm max}}\kappa\rho\,t\,dv'
        \propto \kappa t^{-2}v^{\alpha+1},
    \end{equation}
    where the outer-boundary term is negligible for $\alpha<-1$ and
    $v\ll v_{\rm max}$. With $t_{\rm diff}\sim\tau vt/c$, the diffusion surface
    $t_{\rm diff}=t$ follows
    \begin{equation}
        v_{\rm diff}(t)\propto
        \left(\frac{t^2}{\kappa}\right)^{1/(\alpha+2)}.
    \end{equation}
    The mass exterior to this surface is $M(>v_{\rm diff})\propto
    v_{\rm diff}^{\alpha+3}$ for $\alpha<-3$. While the diffusion surface is inside the
    power-law envelope, the luminosity is therefore
    \begin{equation}
        L(t)\simeq \dot{\epsilon}(t)M(>v_{\rm diff})
        \propto
        \kappa^{-(\alpha+3)/(\alpha+2)}
        t^{-\beta+2(\alpha+3)/(\alpha+2)} .
        \label{eq:kilonova_powerlaw}
    \end{equation}
    For our $\alpha=-4.5$ profile and $\beta=1.3$ heating law,
    $L\propto\kappa^{-0.6}t^{-0.1}$, so the luminosity is almost constant while the
    diffusion surface moves through the power-law envelope. This is the same physical scaling used
    in analytic kilonova models \cite{waxman2018constraints}. It explains both features of the
    kilonova panel in \figref{fig:comparison}: each composition produces an extended luminosity
    plateau, while increasing $X_{\rm lan}$ raises $\kappa$ and lowers the plateau approximately as
    $\kappa^{-0.6}$. Once the ejecta become transparent,
    all of the deposited energy escapes and $L$ instead approaches
    $M_{\rm ej}\dot{\epsilon}\propto t^{-1.3}$, as seen after approximately ten days in
    \figref{fig:comparison}.

   

\section{Summary}
\label{SecSummary}

We introduced the effective expansion opacity,
$\kappa_{\rm exp}=1/(\rho\Delta x)$, based on the distance an outward-travelling photon covers
before reaching optical depth unity in homologous expansion. Taking its Rosseland mean defines
$\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$, a gray transport opacity that accounts for bound-bound interactions
encountered as the photon redshifts. Unlike the ordinary Rosseland mean of narrow lines,
$\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$ converges with frequency resolution. Its ratio to the Planck mean varies
strongly with temperature and density and reaches approximately $10^2$ for the kilonova
composition, showing that the Planck mean is not a reliable transport-opacity proxy.

Single-group URILIGHT calculations using $\kappa_R^{\rm exp}$ reproduce the main light-curve trends
of multigroup calculations for Type~Ia supernova and three kilonova compositions spanning four
decades in $X_{\rm lan}$. The kilonova peak luminosities differ by $7$--$33\%$, while allowing the
calculation to run faster by orders of magnitude.

For kilonova ejecta, we constructed an eight-element composition from the solar $r$-process
residual pattern and varied its lanthanide mass fraction from $X_{\rm lan}=10^{-6}$ to $10^{-1}$.
Following the temperature, density, and opacity sampled by the diffusion region gives
representative opacities from $0.36$ to $18.45$~cm$^2$/g. For the adopted composition and atomic
data, the tested ejecta profiles are approximately described by
$\kappa_{\rm avg}=0.34+99X_{\rm lan}^{0.72}$~cm$^2$/g. These coefficients are model dependent and
should not be interpreted as a universal opacity prescription. The effective opacity remains
nearly constant during most of the diffusion-dominated phase, explaining the luminosity plateau
through the scaling $L\propto\kappa^{-0.6}t^{-0.1}$.

The Python code for building opacity tables using this algorithm is available at 
\url{https://github.com/roee2000/expansion_opacity_calculation}.
